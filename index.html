About this portfolio

A record of how our team planned and built GrandCaddy using Agile, and what I learned doing it.

The project

GrandCaddy is a community-based digital platform that connects people aged 60 and above with trusted, verified helpers (called Caddies) for everyday tasks such as cleaning, grocery shopping, gardening, painting and minor home repairs. Customers post a task with its details, location, date, time and the amount they are willing to pay, and an approved Caddy accepts it. The business uses a pay-per-task model, and its value proposition is Real people, trusted service and simple usage. Family members and caregivers are a secondary customer segment, and helpers must be approved before they provide services (GrandCaddy Team, 2026a). The solution is a responsive, three-tier website made up of a presentation layer, an application layer and a relational data layer (GrandCaddy Team, 2026b).

My part in the project

  • Caddy/Helper user story (User Story 2): defined how a helper registers, builds a profile, browses available tasks, views task details and accepts or rejects a task (14–17 July).
  • Wireframes: created the application wireframes, defined the Caddy profile structure and set out criteria so the team's wireframes stayed consistent (21–24 July).
  • Functionality and testing: added functionality to the homepage, helper and client wireframes, then tested how the pages work together (28–31 July and 4 August).
  • Database: created the project database and connected it to the application so user information is stored safely (4–7 August).

1. Agile Team Charter

An Agile team charter is a living, co-created social contract that defines a team's shared purpose, values, working agreements and boundaries (CPUT, 2026). Ours is built from the principles in the GrandCaddy business model and from the way we actually worked, as recorded in the logbook.

The GrandCaddy Team Charter

PurposeTo design and build GrandCaddy, a website where elderly people can safely and easily book trusted helpers for everyday tasks.
Shared valuesSimplicity, accessibility, safety and trust. The business model is designed around exactly these four ideas (GrandCaddy Team, 2026a), so every design and technical decision is checked against them.

Working agreements
  1. Work only on items that are on the board. Good ideas that are not yet planned, such as verification and ratings, are recorded as future requirements and are not built on the side.
  2. Each member writes a stand-up entry in the logbook every working day: what I completed, what I plan next, and what is blocking me.
  3. The team answers four walkthrough questions at the start of each week: what must we do to advance the work, is anyone working off the board, what must we finish, and what is blocking the flow.
  4. The user interface stays simple. Large buttons, clear labels and simple navigation are required because the primary users are elderly (GrandCaddy Team, 2026b).
  5. A piece of work is shared with the team for opinions before it is called final.
  6. The team moves to the next phase together, only once everyone agrees the current phase is ready.
Definition of doneAn item is done when (a) it meets its acceptance criteria, (b) the team has reviewed it, (c) it works with the other pages it connects to, and (d) it has been tested.

Boundaries and scopeIn scope: customer journey, Caddy journey, administrator features, database and its connection to the application. Recorded as later requirements: full verification, ratings and reviews, payment and notification functionality.
Handling disagreementRaise concerns openly in the stand-up, test the options against the shared values (simple, accessible, safe, trusted), and decide as a team before moving on.

Reflection

What the charter taught me

What happened

At the start the team agreed what GrandCaddy was for, who it was for and how we would work. Because the business model already named simplicity, accessibility, safety and trust, the charter could build directly on those values.

What I thought and felt

A charter can feel like paperwork at the start. That changed for me when I noticed how often the logbook returned to the same worries: too much on one screen, and the team not being aligned. Those were exactly the things the charter was written to prevent.

What worked and what did not

The agreement to record unplanned ideas as future requirements worked well. It kept verification and ratings from pulling us away from the Caddy journey. What worked less well was that keep it simple stayed a general value. It did not tell us how to make every member's wireframes match, so on 21 July I flagged balancing everyone's wireframes as a risk and wrote wireframe criteria to close the gap.

What I learnt

A charter is only useful if it is specific enough to settle an argument. Keep it simple is a value, but one helper per accepted task and no longer visible to others is a rule that I could actually design against.

What I will do next

In my next team I will add an explicit decision rule and a review of the charter after the first sprint, so that it stays a living document, as the brief describes (CPUT, 2026).

2. User Stories and Tasks

A user story is a short description of a software feature written from the end user's perspective; the work behind it is then broken into tasks (CPUT, 2026; Cohn, 2004).

Evidence

The three user stories

User Story 1: Elderly Customer

As an elderly customer, I want to post a task and choose a trusted helper using a simple screen, so that I can get help at home without struggling with technology.

Board items: homepage, post task, task details, accessible navigation, Caddy selection, reviews, ratings, customer support.

User Story 2: Caddy/Helper (my story)

As a Caddy, I want to register, set up my profile, see available tasks and accept or reject them, so that I can earn money helping people in my community.

Board items: Caddy onboarding, profile, available tasks, task details, accept/reject task, verification, ratings, reviews, earnings.

User Story 3: Administrator

As an administrator, I want to approve helpers, monitor tasks and payments and handle disputes, so that customers and their families can trust the platform.

Board items: dashboard, user management, task monitoring, helper approval, verification document review, approve/reject helper, dispute management, payment monitoring.

User Story 2 in detail (the story I wrote)

The business model identifies helpers as a core segment who must be approved before they provide services, with background checks and ratings improving safety and quality (GrandCaddy Team, 2026a). I split my story into five smaller stories so that each could be finished, tested and demonstrated in one pass.

IDStoryAcceptance criteria
US-2.1As a Caddy, I want a simple onboarding flow, so that I can join without a complicated registration.
  • Registration asks only for the information needed to build trust.
  • Each step is on one screen with clear labels.
  • The Caddy is told clearly that approval is required before work begins.
US-2.2As a Caddy, I want to create a profile, so that customers can see who I am and decide whether to trust me.
  • Profile holds: name, picture, description, services offered, location, availability, experience, rating and verification status.
  • Fields that are not yet verified are clearly marked as such.
US-2.3As a Caddy, I want to browse available tasks, so that I can choose work that suits me.
  • Only open tasks are listed.
  • A task that has been accepted by one Caddy is no longer shown to other Caddies.
US-2.4As a Caddy, I want to see the task details, so that I can make an informed decision.
  • Shows task category, description, location, date and time, and payment offered.
  • Shows enough information to decide, without crowding the screen.
US-2.5As a Caddy, I want to accept or reject a task, so that I stay in control of my own work.
  • Accept and reject are two clearly different buttons.
  • A confirmation message follows each action.
  • An accepted task is assigned to exactly one Caddy.

Acceptance criteria are drawn from the requirements recorded in the 14–17 July logbook: onboarding, profile, available tasks, task details, accept/reject workflow, and the risk that one task must go to one helper only.

Reflection

Writing the Caddy user story

What happened

Over four days I turned the team's notes into a complete Caddy story, then used it as the basis for the wireframes and later the database. I finished it on 17 July.

What I thought and felt

On 16 July I worried that my team might not like what I created and that I would have to go back to the drawing board. That worry made me consult them before calling it final, which is why the story held up.

What worked and what did not

Writing from the Caddy's point of view made the difficult trade-off visible: registration must be simple, but the profile must still create trust. Splitting the story into five parts worked well. What was harder was deciding how much information to show on one screen; I raised it on both 15 and 22 July.

What I learnt

A good story is the thing the rest of the project hangs from. The profile fields I listed on 23 July (name, picture, description, services, location, availability, experience, rating, verification status) later told me what the database needed to store, and the accept/reject criterion shaped how task status works.

What I will do next

I will write acceptance criteria at the same time as the story, not after, and test each story with a real user. For GrandCaddy that should include an elderly customer and a helper, because I wrote these stories from the team's notes and research.

Reflection

Living with a changing backlog

What happened

The backlog was not fixed. Between mid-July and early August the team split items, merged others and added verification, earnings and customer support as our understanding of the platform grew.

What I thought and felt

At first I expected a backlog to be a one-time list. Seeing items like Verification move from a note in my Caddy story to a full set of admin tasks showed me that refinement is normal and healthy.

What worked and what did not

Keeping verification, ratings and payment as future requirements let us finish a complete Caddy journey first. The weakness is that my wireframes and database now have to make room for features that are not built yet, which was a risk I logged on 17 July.

What I learnt

Prioritising is mostly about trust and dependency, not about what is easiest. Verification has to come before ratings and payments make sense, because both depend on knowing who the helper is.

What I will do next

I will add estimates and a clear definition of ready to each backlog item, and review the backlog formally at the start of each sprint, as the Scrum Guide recommends (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020).

The data layer I built for in Sprint 4

The solution architecture specifies a central relational database with tables for User, Customer, Helper, Administrator, FamilyMember, Task, TaskCategory, Verification, VerificationDocument, Payment, Review, Notification, Dispute and SupportTicket, and security measures including password hashing, role-based access, HTTPS and regular backups (GrandCaddy Team, 2026b). Reflection

Planning and delivering in weekly sprints

What happened

Each week had a clear goal, and each goal built on the last: story, wireframes, functionality and testing, then the database. Most weeks finished what was planned. The exception was the database connection, which ran into a second day.

What I thought and felt

The database sprint was the most pressured. On 6 August I wrote that I ran into some issues yesterday and that the delay risked too much time away from other components. I was worried about slowing the team.

What worked and what did not

Having a single goal per week stopped the sprints becoming a pile of unrelated tasks. What did not work was estimating: on 5 August I planned to connect the application that day, and the connection was only finished on 7 August, after issues on the first attempt.

What I learnt

A sprint backlog is a commitment I can only keep if I plan with the unknowns in mind. Connecting systems has more unknowns than familiar work like wireframes, so it needs more buffer.

What I will do next

For unfamiliar work I will plan a short exploratory task first and size the real task after, so that my estimate is based on something. I will also test the database with more simultaneous users before deployment, because that was the open risk on 7 August.

Reflection

What keeping a logbook changed

What happened

I wrote a stand-up entry every working day for four weeks. Looking back over the sixteen entries, you can see the project move from planning to design to build.

What I thought and felt

Writing the third question, what are my impediments?, was uncomfortable at first, because it meant admitting uncertainty. Over time it became the most useful question, because writing a risk down made me do something about it.

What worked and what did not

The daily rhythm worked: small, honest entries made it easy to see progress. The weakness was that some entries were repetitive or too general. The 21 July entry and the 16 July entry both describe finishing the user story, which blurs the timeline.

What I learnt

The impediments table above is the real value of the logbook. I had not planned the four themes, but they appear clearly once the entries are laid side by side. Most of my risks were about people and complexity, not technology.

What I will do next

I will make each entry specific (what, which page, which task) and add a short weekly retrospective to the logbook, because the brief describes retrospective notes as part of the tool (CPUT, 2026). I will also link each entry to the Trello card it relates to.

Connect: bringing it together

The fourth step of the e-portfolio process is connecting the artefacts and reflections into a coherent whole (CPUT, 2026).

One thread through all five artefacts

  • The charter set the values (simple, accessible, safe, trusted) and the working agreements.
  • Those values shaped the user stories, especially my Caddy story, whose profile fields were chosen to build trust.
  • The stories became the product backlog, ordered so that trust features rank above convenience.
  • The backlog was cut into four sprints: story, wireframes, functionality and testing, database.
  • The logbook recorded what happened day by day and exposed the same four risks that the charter was meant to manage.

How I grew

I started the project writing requirements from a team's notes. I finished it building a database that stores real users' information. Along the way, my main lesson changed from get the document done to make decisions that the next step can build on. The Caddy profile structure is the clearest example: one planning decision on 23 July became a wireframe, then a set of fields, then part of the data layer.

What I am taking forward

  • Write acceptance criteria with the story.
  • Give unfamiliar work extra time and an exploratory task first.
  • Make charter agreements specific enough to settle disagreements.
  • Review the backlog and charter at the start of every sprint.
